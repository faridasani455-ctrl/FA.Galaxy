Star

Stars are giant balls of hot gas – mostly hydrogen, with some helium and small amounts of other elements. Every star has its own life cycle, ranging from a few million to trillions of years. Astronomers estimate that the universe could contain up to one septillion stars – that’s a one followed by 24 zeros. Our Milky Way alone contains more than 100 billion, also including our most well-studied star, the Sun.

Birth

Stars form in large clouds of gas and dust called molecular clouds. Molecular clouds range from 1,000 to 10 million times the mass of the Sun and can span as much as hundreds of light-years. Molecular clouds are cold which causes gas to clump, creating high-density pockets, eventually, gravity causes some of these clumps to collapse. When this happens, friction causes the material to heat up, which eventually leads to the development of a protostar – a baby star

Life

At first, most of the protostar’s energy comes from heat released by its initial collapse. After millions of years, immense pressures and temperatures in the star’s core squeeze the nuclei of hydrogen atoms together to form helium, a process called nuclear fusion. Nuclear fusion releases energy, which heats the star and prevents it from further collapsing under the force of gravity. These stars undergoing such change is called main sequence star. The star’s luminosity, size, and temperature will slowly change over millions or billions of years during this phase. Our Sun is roughly midway through its main sequence stage.

Lifespan by Star Type

The life expectancy of a star varies dramatically depending on its size and energy consumption rate .Massive star, the mass of 8 solar masses have a lifespan over few million to a few hundred million years .Medium star , the mass of 1 solar mass have a lifespan over 10 billion years. Low-Mass star , , the mass of 0.5 solar masses have a lifespan over 100 billion to >10 trillion years.

Death

At the beginning of the end of a star’s life, its core runs out of hydrogen to convert into helium. Also, the energy produced by fusion creates pressure inside the star that balances gravity’s tendency to pull matter together, so the core starts to collapse. However, the steps of the late stages of the star’s death depends strongly on its mass. A low-mass star’s atmosphere will keep expanding until it becomes a giant star while during the process, fusion converts helium into carbon in the core. An example is our sun, in serval billion years. All that’s left of the star is its core, now called a white dwarf, a roughly Earth-sized stellar cinder that gradually cools over billions of years. The star’s iron core collapses until forces between the nuclei push the brakes, then it rebounds. This creates a shock wave that travels outward through the star, as result there will be a big explosion; namely supernova. Later on, the core will survies as either a black hole or neutron star.

Neutron stars

Neutron stars are stellar remnants that pack more mass than the Sun into a sphere about as wide as New York City’s Manhattan Island is long. It is created, when a main sequence star with between about eight and 20 times the Sun’s mass runs out of hydrogen in its core.

Black Hole

Black holes are among the most mysterious cosmic objects in our univers, much studied but not fully understood . These objects aren’t really holes. They’re huge concentrations of matter packed into very tiny spaces.It is said that “ black holes are so dense that gravity just beneath its surface, the event horizon, is strong enough that nothing – not even light – can escape." The event horizon isn’t a surface like Earth’s or even the Sun’s. It’s a boundary that contains all the matter that makes up the black hole.
